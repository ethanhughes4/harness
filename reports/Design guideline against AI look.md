# Make every choice argue for its client

The "AI look" is not one bad choice. It is a stack of unchosen defaults appearing together: Inter or system-ui as the only typeface, an indigo-to-purple gradient, a centred hero with a pill badge, three identical icon-topped cards, one radius and one shadow on everything, glows and glass, all-caps eyebrow labels, emoji icons, and an app that only designs the happy path. The only large measurement found, a DOM scan of about 1,590 Show HN sites, put **22% at four or more of these tells and another 32% at two or three** ([Krebs](https://www.adriankrebs.ch/blog/design-slop/)). Acclaimed products point the other way. Linear, Vercel, Things, IBM Carbon and GOV.UK describe beauty as the result of constraint: one or two type families, a neutral-first palette with one accent, stepped scales where every step has a job, and fixed presets for radius and shadow. Each spends its distinctiveness on one or two owned signatures. For an autonomous pipeline serving many clients, the key finding is that **ban lists only move the model to the next default**. Anthropic's own skill now lists the "tasteful" escape routes (cream with serif and terracotta, near-black with an acid-green accent, hairline broadsheet layouts) as tells in their own right ([anthropics/skills SKILL.md](https://github.com/anthropics/skills/blob/main/skills/frontend-design/SKILL.md)). So the guideline has to require a written reason, tied to the client's brief, for every identity-bearing choice. It also has to check that the reason was actually built, because generative tools leave over a quarter of their stated design rationales unimplemented ([Imteyaz et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22928)). Scripts can gate most craft rules on a running page. A screenshot judge is reliable only for pairwise comparisons with large quality gaps and for narrow yes/no questions.

## Four or more co-occurring defaults is the real tell

Designers and tool authors agree on a core set of tells to an unusual degree. Krebs's Playwright scorer used deterministic CSS and DOM checks rather than model judgement, and estimated 5–10% false positives. It checked for: Inter everywhere; the font pairings models fall back on (Space Grotesk, Instrument Serif, Geist); a serif italic accent word inside an Inter hero; "VibeCode Purple"; permanent dark mode with grey body text; gradients and coloured glows; a centred hero with a badge above the H1; coloured top or left borders on cards; identical icon-topped feature cards; numbered 1-2-3 steps; stat banner rows; emoji in navigation; all-caps headings; unmodified shadcn/ui; and glassmorphism ([Krebs](https://www.adriankrebs.ch/blog/design-slop/)). The same items appear independently in Impeccable's 67-entry catalogue, which splits its rules into **49 source-checkable, 12 browser-checkable and 6 needing design review** ([impeccable](https://impeccable.style/anti-patterns)). They also appear in the avoid-ai-design scanner, which ranks tells P0/P1/P2 and fails a build on any P0 or P1 ([GitHub](https://github.com/funboy322/avoid-ai-design)), and in practitioner guides from [Mania Design](https://www.mania.design/blog/spot-the-slop-a-ui-designers-guide-to-fixing-ai-defaults/) and [925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/ai-slop-web-design-guide). NN/g tested ten tools (v0, Lovable, Bolt, Claude, Stitch and others) on a real redesign. It found screens that "appear flat and interchangeable", with weak hierarchy and grouping, overused colour, poor contrast and inconsistent margins: "good from afar, but far from good" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-prototyping/)). An academic benchmark of 120 interfaces from five tools found "convergence in visual appearance and layout organization", while colour varied more ([arXiv 2607.22928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22928)).

The cause is structural, not a matter of taste. Anthropic calls it **distributional convergence**: "Safe design choices–those that work universally and offend no one–dominate web training data", so any decision the prompt leaves open gets filled with the median ([Claude blog](https://www.claude.com/blog/improving-frontend-design-through-skills)). Framework defaults amplify the median. Adam Wathan joked that making Tailwind UI buttons `bg-indigo-500` "caused every AI-generated interface on Earth to turn purple" ([X](https://x.com/adamwathan/status/1953510802159219096)). That is commentary, not a measured cause, but it matches the indigo lineage the scanners look for. The important consequence is that bans regress. Anthropic's 2025 prompt banned Inter and purple and already warned that models then converge on Space Grotesk. The current skill instead targets the clichés the first round of anti-slop prompts produced: cream and terracotta (#D97757), near-black with a neon accent, broadsheet hairlines, the uniform SaaS-card kit, and "template chrome" such as eyebrows, middle-dot meta strings, arrows appended to links, and 01/02/03 markers on content that isn't a sequence ([SKILL.md](https://github.com/anthropics/skills/blob/main/skills/frontend-design/SKILL.md)). Its framing is the right one for a pipeline: "All traits are legitimate for some briefs, but they are defaults rather than choices." Mania makes the same point from practice: a brand whose logo really is purple "gets a pass" ([Mania](https://www.mania.design/blog/spot-the-slop-a-ui-designers-guide-to-fixing-ai-defaults/)).

Two consequences follow. First, a single tell is weak evidence and co-occurrence is the signal, so checks should score totals and treat one justified instance as acceptable. Second, the ban list must be a living, versioned file. A fixed list goes stale within a model generation.

## App tells are structural: the happy path and the equal grid

In product UI the tells shift from decoration to structure. A dashboard guide lists six default failures: cards used to decorate rather than group, the equal three-up KPI grid, proportional numerals in tables, gradients on status colours, missing empty, loading and error states, and "live" indicators on everything, capped at **two per viewport** ([Laith0003 wiki](https://github.com/Laith0003/ux-skill/wiki/How-to-design-a-dashboard-with-Claude-Code)). Mania sums up the pattern: "AI nails the happy path every time" and shows "everything, all at once" ([Mania](https://www.mania.design/blog/spot-the-slop-a-ui-designers-guide-to-fixing-ai-defaults/)). The benchmark data is harsher. **Four of five generative UI tools implemented 6% or fewer of functional UX principles**, and **34% of functional rationales the tools stated were never implemented** ([arXiv 2607.22928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22928)). That second figure matters directly here. If the pipeline requires a stated reason for each choice, a reviewer has to verify that the reason shows up in the build. Otherwise the reasons become what the paper calls "design theater".

Copy has its own tells, and they reinforce the visual ones. Common examples are interchangeable headlines ("Build the future of work"), buzzword verbs (unlock, unleash, supercharge, empower, world-class), forced contrasts ("Not a feature. A platform.") and an em-dash in every sentence ([925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/ai-slop-web-design-guide); [Microcopy Examples](https://microcopyexamples.substack.com/p/avoid-landing-page-words); [impeccable](https://impeccable.style/anti-patterns)). The practical test is whether a competitor could use the line unchanged. Stripe's "Financial infrastructure for the internet" passes and "Scale without limits" does not ([925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/ai-slop-web-design-guide)). Fabricated stats and fake logo walls are named as credibility damage in the vendored web-design-engineer skill. The notes found no independent source naming them as an AI tell, so treat that rule as integrity, not detection.

## Acclaimed design is a tight system plus one owned signature

The teams behind widely admired product UI describe systems, not inspiration. Linear rebuilt theme generation in LCH so that "a red and a yellow color with lightness 50 will appear roughly equally light". It cut each theme from **98 variables to three inputs** (base, accent, contrast), reduced chrome colour for "a more neutral and timeless appearance", and raised text contrast in both modes ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)). Vercel's Geist has 10 colour scales of 10 steps each. Every step has a job: **100–300 backgrounds, 400–600 borders, 700–800 high-contrast fills, 900 secondary text, 1000 primary text**. Radii, fills, strokes and shadows are presets, and gray is the default for nearly everything ([Geist colors](https://vercel.com/geist/colors.md); [Geist intro](https://vercel.com/geist/introduction)). IBM Carbon builds on an **8-pixel mini unit** with the spacing scale 8, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 80 ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/2x-grid/overview/)). Things, in a third-party reading, uses an off-white ground, system sans and **one cobalt accent**, with no gradient and no brand-colour fill ([shadcn.io](https://www.shadcn.io/design/things)). GOV.UK's principles add the caveat that keeps systems from turning into sameness: "Be consistent, not uniform", and "Making something look simple is easy. Making something simple to use is much harder" ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles)).

Typography has the firmest numbers. Butterick gives body text of **15–25 px on the web**, line spacing of **120–145%**, line length of **45–90 characters**, and **5–12% extra tracking for all caps**, which should be used for less than one line ([Practical Typography](https://practicaltypography.com/summary-of-key-rules.html)). Acclaimed products use one superfamily plus at most a mono: Geist Sans and Mono at Vercel, Inter Display for headings with Inter for everything else at Linear ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)). The vendors' own guardrails converge on the same limits: at most two font families, a palette of three to six colours, and no decorative gradients or blobs ([leaked v0 prompt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/x1xhlol/system-prompts-and-models-of-ai-tools/main/v0%20Prompts%20and%20Tools/Prompt.txt); [SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anthropics/skills/main/skills/frontend-design/SKILL.md)).

Distinctiveness lives in a few places. Linear kept Apple conventions for structure and spent its expression on Inter Display headings and generated themes. Stripe's gradient, Vercel's visible grid, Things' restraint and CapWords' sticker interaction (an Apple Design Award 2025 winner) are each one ownable move against an otherwise disciplined system ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2025/06/apple-unveils-winners-and-finalists-of-the-2025-apple-design-awards/)). That a few signatures are enough is an inference from these examples, not a measured result. The examples also carry a warning. The Linear look itself (dark ground, blurred gradient behind a product shot, animated glow) has been copied so widely that critics now call it "the statistical middle" of recent SaaS ([UX Collective Bootcamp](https://bootcamp.uxdesign.cc/the-rise-of-linear-style-design-origins-trends-and-techniques-4fd96aab7646); [LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/ux-design/linear-design)). A pipeline that sends every developer-tool client to "the Linear recipe" rebuilds the convergence problem one level up. Award-winning marketing spectacle (Awwwards SOTY 2025 went to WebGL-heavy work such as Lando Norris by OFF+BRAND.) pulls in a different direction from acclaimed product UI. Client apps should copy the product-UI traits, not the spectacle ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/websites/%23EDF726/?page=5)).

Research ties two of these traits to how people judge a page. Colourfulness and visual complexity are the most noticeable properties at first sight. Screenshot-based models of them, with demographics, explain **about half the variance in appeal after a 500 ms view**, and preferred levels vary by culture ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)). That supports controlling palette size and clutter. It does not support a single universal aesthetic target.

## Agencies turn a brief into "this, but not that"

Studios that produce a distinct identity per client run a short, repeatable discovery. Samantha Warren's Style Tiles interview asks stakeholders to rank visual goals, answer metaphor questions ("If your website was a vehicle, what vehicle would it be and why?") and place the brand on word pairs. The adjectives used most often go to the top of the list ([A List Apart](https://alistapart.com/article/style-tiles-and-how-they-work/)). ATEN's "This, But Not That" exercise starts with **three to seven adjectives**, pairs each with the adjective where it goes too far ("emotive but not sensational"), and votes down to the two strongest ([ATEN](https://atendesigngroup.com/articles/not)). Personality sliders (Serious–Playful, Conventional–Rebel, Friend–Authority and so on) push away from the middle, because "true brand personality often lies at the ends of these spectrums" ([Learning Loop](https://learningloop.io/plays/workshop-exercise/personality-sliders)). Agencies then audit competitors to "identify gaps or opportunities to differentiate" ([KOTA](https://kota.co.uk/blog/how-to-build-successful-brands)). An agent with only a written brief can run all of this itself. The "but not" column is the natural home for the anti-default guard: "modern but not sterile SaaS", "trustworthy but not navy-and-gold bank".

Mapping adjectives to visual choices has partial evidence. Typeface personality follows type class: serif reads traditional, sans casual, and monospace plain ([Shaikh et al.](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/perception-of-fonts-perceived-personality-traits-and-uses/)). A typeface congruent with the content speeds responses and shifts the perceived emotional tone ([Hazlett et al.](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2021/06/Hazlett-Larson-Shaikh-Chaparo-2013-two-studies-on-how-a-typeface-congruent-with-content-can-enhance-onscreen-communication.pdf)). Hue maps onto Aaker's brand-personality dimensions (Sincerity, Excitement, Competence, Sophistication, Ruggedness), and **saturation and value amplify those traits** ([Labrecque & Milne](https://colab.ws/articles/10.1007%2Fs11747-010-0245-y); [Aaker](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/working-papers/dimensions-brand-personality)). The exact hue table sits behind a paywall and was not verified. No validated framework maps adjectives to layout density or motion, so those mappings are rules of thumb. Distinctiveness research adds a caution: colour is the type of brand asset **least likely to be unique and famous** ([Marketing Science](https://www.marketingscience.info/brands-need-distinctive-assets)). A direction that differs from a competitor only by hue has not differentiated.

How many directions to show is genuinely contested. Warren used three tiles, each led by one adjective ("clean", "strong", "friendly"). KOTA develops four or five identity routes. Parachute argues that a web agency "should present one recommended direction", because menus produce "a diluted, assembled result". It makes an exception for brand identity work ([Parachute](https://parachutedesign.ca/blog/how-many-design-concepts-should-an-agency-present/)). The two positions reconcile for a pipeline: show about three, mark one as recommended with written reasoning, and expect mix-and-match feedback. Warren's client wanted "the first tile" with "the red stars from the second" and "the typeface from the third", and the designer resolved that into one coherent tile that "translated directly to the final design" ([A List Apart](https://alistapart.com/article/style-tiles-and-how-they-work/)). NN/g gives the difference test: options must differ enough to be "immediately detectable to a nondesigner", and should be shown in randomised order ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/testing-visual-design/)). Style tiles and element collages, which show real components such as buttons, forms and a data row rather than full pages, are the cheapest artifacts an agent can render, screenshot and critique ([Dan Mall](https://v3.danmall.com/articles/rif-element-collages)).

## Scripts gate the craft; model judges rank only wide gaps

Most craft rules can be computed. Contrast, target size, focus visibility, the number of distinct font sizes, colours, spacings, radii and shadows, spacing-scale adherence, gradient hues, `background-clip: text`, side-tab borders, emoji, motion durations and reduced-motion handling can all be read from computed styles and bounding boxes. Two hard references anchor this: WCAG contrast of **4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large text and UI boundaries** ([W3C 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); [W3C 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)), and targets of **at least 24×24 CSS px** ([W3C 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)), with 44 pt and 48 dp on Apple and Android touch platforms ([Android](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/7101858)). Automation still has a ceiling. Axe fully covered **57% of accessibility issues by volume** across 13,000+ pages ([Deque](https://www.deque.com/blog/automated-testing-study-identifies-57-percent-of-digital-accessibility-issues)), and computed aesthetic metrics explain only **36% of the variance in aesthetic ratings for mobile UIs and up to about half for webpages** ([Oulasvirta et al.](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/30261274/ELEC_oulasvirta_et_al_2018_002_.pdf)). Count thresholds such as "eight or fewer font sizes" come from practice and have never been validated against perceived professionalism. They work as regression guards, not as taste scores.

Vision-model judges are reliable in a narrow band. In "MLLM as a UI Judge", Claude and GPT-4o agreed with humans on pairwise preferences **about 60% of the time overall**. That rose to **about 93% when the human score gap was large** and stayed near chance for close pairs and for ease of use, which the models overrated. Exact Likert agreement was only 35–38%, and model scores clustered more tightly than human ones ([arXiv 2510.08783](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08783v1)). ArtifactsBench reports 94.4% ranking consistency with WebDev Arena using per-task checklists over interaction screenshots, but these are the authors' own figures and have not been replicated ([arXiv 2507.04952](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04952)). Switching from pointwise to pairwise judging raised accuracy in visual generation ([GenArena](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.06013)). Position bias persists ([arXiv 2606.05697](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.05697v1)). Few-shot prompting with designer-critiqued examples improved LLM UI feedback by **55%** ([UICrit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08850)). Anthropic's long-running harness found that agents "reliably skew positive when grading their own work". It therefore kept the evaluator separate, calibrated it with scored examples, weighted design quality and originality above craft, and found that scores plateaued, sometimes leaving a middle iteration as the best one ([Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). No study has tested whether VLM judges are biased *toward* the AI look itself. That is the largest open risk in using a model to police AI defaults.

## Conclusion

Every source treats the AI look as an absence of decisions more than a style. That changes what a pipeline should enforce. A list of forbidden fonts and colours is necessary but self-defeating on its own: models route around it to the next median, and the list has already had to be rewritten once. The durable mechanism has three parts. First, every identity-bearing choice carries a reason traceable to a brief adjective or a competitor gap. Second, a reviewer that is not the builder verifies that each reason is visible in the running page. Third, scripted checks keep the craft floor high enough that the judge's limited attention goes to the questions only it can answer: is this coherent, is it distinct from its siblings, does it read as the adjectives it claims.

The open risk is evaluator taste. No one has measured whether vision models prefer the AI look, and the evidence says they cannot separate close variants. The safest use of a judge is therefore adversarial and comparative. Render the same content with no direction as a "generic baseline". Then require the judge, in both presentation orders, to tell the client direction apart from that baseline and from the other clients' directions, and to match it to its own adjective list. A direction that a model cannot tell from the default is not distinctive, whatever its hex codes say.

## The guideline

Evidence labels: **[E]** evidence-backed (a standard or a measured study), **[C]** consensus (several independent practitioner or vendor sources agree), **[R]** rule of thumb (practice or inference, unvalidated). **Overriding rule [C]:** a Never item may be used, and an Always item may be broken, only when the brand sheet records a reason that names the brief, the client's existing assets, or a competitor gap. "It looks modern/clean/premium" is not a reason. The reviewer checks that each recorded reason is visible in the build, because tools leave 25–34% of stated rationales unimplemented ([arXiv 2607.22928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22928)).

### 1. Never — the AI tells

Score tells per page. **Four or more unjustified tells fail the build; two or three trigger review [R; Krebs's tiers measure prevalence, not human judgement].** A single tell with a recorded reason passes.

| N19 | Fabricated stats, fake logos, dummy testimonials | Damages credibility | Code: numbers or logos not present in the brief or assets | "Real data needed" placeholder | C (integrity) |

### 3. Per client — from brief to a distinct direction

**Step 1. Extract facts [C].** From the brief, record: product and category, audience and context of use, primary job of the site or app, existing assets (logo, product screenshots, photography, mandated colours or fonts), and constraints. Missing logo or product imagery is a blocking question for the owner, not something to draw.

**Step 2. Answer the discovery questions in writing [C].** The agent answers from the brief and logs every inference as an assumption. Where the brief is silent, it picks a side and records it rather than defaulting to the middle.
- Rank the three outcomes the design must achieve.
- If this brand were a vehicle (or a place, or a person), what would it be, and why?
- Where does it sit on each word pair: modern–traditional, illustrative–typographic, dense–spacious?
- What must the audience trust before they act?
- What must this never be mistaken for? Name a category cliché and an AI default.
- Who are three or more competitors or alternatives, and what do they all look like?

**Step 3. Write "this, not that" adjectives [C].** Write three to seven adjectives, each with a "but not" overshoot, then rank them and keep the top two or three. At least one "but not" names the category default ("trustworthy but not navy-and-gold bank") and one names an AI default ("modern but not dark-glow SaaS"). Also write the NN/g verification list: the target words, their opposites and distractors [E-method].

**Step 4. Place the sliders [C].** Use about six sliders (Serious–Playful, Conventional–Rebel, Elite–Mass, Friend–Authority, Classic–Innovative, Quiet–Loud), placed toward the ends with a one-line justification each. Rate the competitors on the same sliders.

**Step 5. Audit the competitor look [C].** Screenshot or describe three or more competitors. List the category conventions (palette, type class, layout, imagery). Tag each as *keep* (a trust cue the audience needs, such as clear pricing or regulated disclosures) or *break*. Each direction must break at least one convention on purpose.

**Step 6. Map attributes to choices, each with a reason.** For every row, write "chosen X because adjective/slider/gap Y".

| Lever | Mapping | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Adjectives → Aaker dimension → type class: serif for traditional/sophisticated, sans for casual/approachable, mono for technical/plain; a display face carries the lead adjective | E (type class), R (via Aaker) |
| Colour | Lead adjective → hue family; saturation and value set intensity (higher amplifies excitement, lower reads sophisticated or competent); one accent; neutrals tinted toward the accent | E (direction, Labrecque & Milne) / R (specific values) |
| Layout and density | Elite–Mass and Quiet–Loud → whitespace and section padding (marketing 48–128 px; dashboards 4–12 px gaps); Conventional–Rebel → grid symmetry | R |
| Shape | Serious–Playful → radius and stroke weight | R |
| Imagery | Illustrative–typographic word pair; real product UI for digital products; client photography over stock | C / R |
| Motion | Quiet–Loud → zero or one orchestrated moment; Classic–Innovative → easing character | R |
| Ownable assets | Name two or more non-colour assets (type pairing, graphic device, shape language, signature interaction), since colour alone is the weakest distinctive asset | E (Romaniuk) / R |

**Step 7. Make three directions that differ [C].** Each direction is led by a different top adjective and comes from a different school (web-design-engineer's school table works for this). A style recipe may supply starting values, but at least the display type, accent and one layout move must trace to this brief. Mark one direction as recommended, with written reasoning [C, Parachute].

**Step 8. Test that they are different enough [E-method / R].**
- A non-designer would tell them apart at a glance. A hue swap on the same layout fails.
- Code check: directions differ in display family, in accent hue by more than 60°, and in at least one layout primitive (hero composition, grid, density).
- Screenshot judge check, in both orders: given the three adjective lists, can it match each preview to its list? Can it tell each from a no-direction baseline of the same content?

**Step 9. Show visual previews and let the owner choose [C].**
- Render each direction as a style tile or element collage. Use the client's real copy and real components: header, primary and secondary button, form field with an error, a card, a data row, and an imagery treatment.
- Screenshot each at desktop and mobile widths and show them in randomised order.
- Put the lead adjective, its "but not", and a three-line rationale under each.
- The owner picks one or asks for a mix. The agent resolves a mix into one coherent tile, does not stitch parts together, and re-previews.

**Step 10. Write the brand sheet [C].** The approved tile becomes the source for tokens. The sheet holds:
- positioning line
- adjectives with their "but not"s
- slider positions for the client and its competitors
- category conventions tagged keep or break
- chosen direction with its reasoning
- type, palette, spacing, shape and motion tokens, each with its reason
- imagery rules
- ownable assets
- target word list

The builder may not introduce a value that is not on the sheet. The reviewer runs the word-list test against the finished build.

**Running the judge reliably.**
- **Prefer pairwise to scores [E].** Pairwise agreement with humans is about 60% overall, rising to about 93% on large gaps. Absolute Likert scores match exactly only about a third of the time and cluster tightly. Use pairwise for choosing between rounds, between directions, and against the no-direction baseline.
- **Swap positions [E].** Run every pairwise comparison in both orders and accept only verdicts that agree. A disagreement is a tie: no action, keep the incumbent. This handles position bias and close-pair noise.
- **Use checklists, not holistic grades [E].** Use per-task yes/no items, as ArtifactsBench does. Do not ask the judge to rate "ease of use", where its accuracy is near chance. Use scripted checks and ui-ux-pro-max rules for that.
- **Calibrate with examples [E].** Include three to five few-shot examples in the judge prompt: a page with many tells, a good page from the same category, and a borderline one, each with a written critique (the UICrit approach, +55%).
- **Separate the judge from the builder [C].** The judge never grades its own work. Prefer a different model or at least a fresh context.
- **Cap rounds and keep the best [C].** Fix the top three issues per round, for a maximum of three rounds. Keep the best round by pairwise comparison, not the last one. On a plateau, pivot to another direction instead of polishing.
- **Fix the order of checks [C].** Scripts run first and are hard gates. The judge sees only pages that pass the scripts, so its limited reliability goes to coherence, distinctiveness and adjective fit.
- **Keep the ban list live [C].** Version the N-list. When the judge or owner sees a new recurring pattern across clients, such as two clients' directions converging, add it with a detector and date it. Periodically compare finished client sites against each other pairwise. If the judge cannot tell two clients apart, the pipeline has found its next default.
